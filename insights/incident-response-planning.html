Incident Response Planning: What Happens After the Breach

It is 2:47 AM on a Tuesday. Your monitoring tool fires an alert — or more likely, an employee discovers they cannot access their files, and a ransom note is staring back at them from the screen. The next 60 minutes will determine whether this incident costs your business a week of downtime or a year of recovery. And the difference comes down to one thing: whether you have a tested incident response plan or whether you are making it up as you go.

Most small and mid-sized businesses fall into the second category. They discover the gaps in their response capabilities during an actual incident — which is the worst possible time to discover anything.

Why Most SMBs Don't Have IR Plans

The reasons are predictable and understandable, but none of them are acceptable.

"We're too small to be a target." This has not been true for years. Automated attacks do not discriminate by company size. Ransomware operators specifically target SMBs because they are more likely to pay and less likely to have defenses.

"Our MSP handles that." Most MSPs do not have incident response capabilities for their clients. They may be able to restore a backup, but they are not equipped to contain an active breach, preserve forensic evidence, coordinate with legal counsel, or manage client notification.

"We'll figure it out when it happens." You will not. Under the pressure of an active incident — with business operations halted, executives demanding answers, and the clock ticking on regulatory notification deadlines — improvisation leads to mistakes that compound the damage.

An incident response plan is not insurance against a breach. It is the operational playbook that determines whether a breach is a setback or an existential threat.

The Anatomy of an IR Plan

An effective incident response plan follows the six-phase framework established by NIST. Each phase has a specific purpose, and skipping any of them leads to predictable failures.

1. Preparation

This is everything you do before an incident occurs. It is the most important phase because it determines your readiness when the call comes. Preparation includes:

  • Defining the IR team — who is on it, what their roles are, and how to reach them at 3 AM
  • Establishing communication channels that do not depend on compromised infrastructure (if your email is down, how does the team coordinate?)
  • Documenting your critical assets, network architecture, and key vendor contacts
  • Pre-engaging legal counsel and a forensics firm so you are not cold-calling during a crisis
  • Establishing relationships with your cyber insurance carrier and understanding your policy's notification requirements

2. Detection and Analysis

How do you know an incident is happening? Detection relies on your monitoring, alerting, and logging capabilities. But detection is only half the equation. Analysis is where you determine the scope and severity of the event: What systems are affected? What data is at risk? Is the attacker still active in the environment? Is this a single compromised endpoint or a domain-wide breach?

The quality of your analysis directly determines the quality of your response. Rushed or incomplete analysis leads to either over-reaction (shutting down everything when only one system is compromised) or under-reaction (treating a major breach as a minor issue).

3. Containment

Once you understand the scope, you stop the bleeding. Containment has two sub-phases: short-term containment (isolate affected systems immediately to prevent spread) and long-term containment (implement temporary fixes that allow partial business operations while you prepare for eradication).

The critical decision in containment is balancing speed against evidence preservation. Pulling the plug on a server stops the attack but may destroy forensic evidence that you need for the investigation — and potentially for legal or regulatory proceedings.

4. Eradication

Remove the threat from your environment entirely. This means identifying the root cause — the initial access vector, the persistence mechanisms, and any backdoors the attacker may have left behind. Eradication without root cause analysis is incomplete. If you do not know how the attacker got in, you cannot be confident they are gone.

5. Recovery

Bring systems back online in a controlled, validated manner. Recovery is not just restoring from backup. It includes verifying system integrity, monitoring for signs of re-compromise, and validating that business operations can resume safely. Rush the recovery and you risk restarting operations on a still-compromised environment.

6. Lessons Learned

Within two weeks of incident closure, conduct a formal post-incident review. What happened? How was it detected? What worked in the response? What failed? What needs to change? This phase is the one most organizations skip, and it is the reason the same types of incidents recur.

The six phases of incident response are not optional steps in a suggested process. They are the difference between a contained incident and a cascading failure.

Tabletop Exercises: Testing Without the Pain

A plan that has never been tested is a theory, not a plan. Tabletop exercises are the most efficient way to validate your IR plan without the cost and stress of a real incident.

A tabletop exercise is a facilitated discussion where the IR team walks through a realistic scenario — ransomware attack, data breach, insider threat, compromised vendor — and talks through their response step by step. There is no simulated attack. No systems are touched. The entire exercise happens in a conference room (or video call) over 60 to 90 minutes.

What tabletops consistently reveal:

  • Contact lists are outdated — key people have changed roles or left the company
  • Communication plans assume infrastructure that would be unavailable during an incident
  • No one knows who has authority to make critical decisions (shut down a production system, engage law enforcement, authorize a ransom payment)
  • Legal notification timelines are not understood — most regulated industries have 72-hour or shorter windows
  • Backup restoration has never been tested at scale

Every one of these gaps is better discovered during a tabletop than during a real incident. Run at least two tabletop exercises per year, with different scenarios each time.

Common IR Mistakes

After supporting organizations through real incidents, the same mistakes appear repeatedly:

  • Wiping systems before forensic imaging: Destroying evidence that you may need for insurance claims, legal proceedings, or regulatory investigations.
  • Communicating over compromised channels: Discussing your response plan over email when email is part of the compromised infrastructure. The attacker is reading your playbook in real time.
  • Delaying notification: Hoping the incident will resolve itself quietly while regulatory notification clocks are ticking. Late notification carries its own penalties.
  • No single point of command: Multiple people making conflicting decisions about containment, communication, and recovery. An IR plan must define a clear incident commander.
  • Skipping the post-mortem: Declaring victory the moment systems are back online and never analyzing what went wrong. This guarantees you will make the same mistakes next time.

The organizations that survive incidents with minimal damage are not the ones with the best technology. They are the ones that practiced before the real thing happened.

Build the Plan Before You Need It

An incident response plan does not need to be a 50-page document. It needs to be a clear, tested, accessible playbook that your team can execute under pressure. It needs current contact lists, defined roles, pre-negotiated vendor relationships, and communication templates ready to deploy.

If your organization does not have an IR plan today, the only acceptable response is to start building one this week. Identify your IR team. Define the decision authorities. Pre-engage legal counsel and a forensics firm. Schedule your first tabletop exercise. These steps take days, not months — and they fundamentally change your ability to survive an incident.

You do not get to choose whether an incident happens. You get to choose whether you are ready for it.
